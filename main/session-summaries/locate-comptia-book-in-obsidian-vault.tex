\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Personal knowledge vaults are frequently used as private study corpora, and the
provenance and internal quality of the material stored in them are rarely
audited. This paper reports a retrieval-and-audit case study conducted on a
single personal Obsidian vault. A case-insensitive recursive search across the
vault root \texttt{\string~/Documents/vmain} returned one matching study
artifact: \emph{CompTIA Security+ Guide to Network Security Fundamentals} by
Mark Ciampa, held in two distinct forms---a 12.2\,MB source PDF and a
directory of extracted per-module notes spanning fifteen modules. The study
scope was then narrowed to Module~7, ``Identity and Access Management,''
aligned to the CompTIA SY0-701 objective domain, and that module was read in
full (471 lines, 21.6\,KB) and decomposed against its three stated learning
objectives: describing authentication credential types, listing authentication
best practices, and defining access control schemes. The audit surfaced two
concrete defects: an internal contradiction between a stated industry
password-expiry practice and an explicit recommendation against expiry, and a
malformed review question offering two textually identical answer options.
The paper closes by specifying a drill protocol in which a learner supplies
examination questions and each is answered with a correctness score and a
line-level citation back into the source note. All findings are descriptive of
the audited artifact; no empirical learner-outcome data are reported.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The effective ceiling of a self-directed certification study programme is set
by the material the learner can reliably find, read, and trust. For many
learners that material does not live in a publisher's learning platform but in
a personal knowledge vault---an Obsidian library that accumulates PDFs, notes,
clippings, and self-authored prose without any editorial layer between the
source document and the study page. Two practical problems follow. First, a
retrieval problem: the learner must locate the authoritative artifact when
the same title exists simultaneously as a binary source and as a tree of
derived notes. Second, and less often confronted, a trust problem: derived
notes are the layer a learner actually reads, yet they are the layer most
likely to contain silent inconsistencies, because no editorial pass has
verified the derived text against the source or against its own internal
consistency.

This paper documents a case study that treats both problems as a single
retrieval-and-quality-assurance exercise. The subject is Module~7,
``Identity and Access Management,'' of a CompTIA Security+ study corpus held
in a personal Obsidian vault and aligned to the SY0-701 objective domain.

The contributions of this case report are threefold: (i) a documented account
of how a single study title was resolved from a vault-root search into two
concrete artifacts, one of which was selected as the study surface; (ii) a
descriptive decomposition of the selected module into its objective-aligned
content blocks, including the attack taxonomy, the multifactor token
distinctions, the biometric modality taxonomy, the digest-protection
mechanisms, the single sign-on technology set, and the ranked access control
schemes; and (iii) a reproducible quality audit that identified two defects in
the note, one logical and one structural. The paper is deliberately
descriptive: it characterises an artifact and a process, and it does not
claim that the audit improves examination outcomes, because no outcome data
were collected.

\section{Background and Scope}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{The audited corpus}

The vault root searched was \texttt{\string~/Documents/vmain}, a personal
Obsidian vault on the author's machine. The search was case-insensitive and
recursive over the vault root, and it was keyed on the study title rather than
on a filename pattern.

Exactly one matching title was returned: \emph{CompTIA Security+ Guide to
Network Security Fundamentals} by Mark Ciampa. The title materialises in two
forms within the vault:

{\renewcommand{\labelenumi}{(\roman{enumi})}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item a source PDF of 12.2\,MB, and
  \item a directory of extracted per-module notes covering fifteen modules.
\end{enumerate}}

This duality is the substantive reason a retrieval step was needed at all. The
PDF is the authoritative container but is poorly suited to targeted revision;
the note tree is the revision surface but is a derived artifact whose fidelity
is unverified. The case study therefore resolved the ambiguity by taking the
note tree as the study surface while retaining the PDF as the provenance
reference.

\subsection{Module under audit}

The learner narrowed scope to Module~7, ``Identity and Access Management,''
associated with the CompTIA SY0-701 objective domain. The module note was
471 lines and 21.6\,KB in size, and was read in full rather than sampled. The
module is organised around three stated objectives, quoted here as they frame
the study material:

{\renewcommand{\labelenumi}{\textbf{O\arabic{enumi}.}}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item describe authentication credential types;
  \item list authentication best practices; and
  \item define access control schemes.
\end{enumerate}}

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

The procedure had four stages: retrieval, scope selection, close reading, and
defect audit. Each stage is reported descriptively.

\paragraph{Stage 1 --- Retrieval.}
A single case-insensitive recursive search was executed over the vault root on
the study title. This stage yields the set of candidate artifacts and, by
reporting them, exposes the PDF/note-tree duality described in
Section~\ref{sec:background}.

\paragraph{Stage 2 --- Scope selection.}
The learner selected one module from the fifteen available. The selection
reduced the audit target from a corpus to a single self-contained note, which
made full reading tractable: the entire note was consumed, not sampled, so
every claim in Section~\ref{sec:results} is grounded in the complete text of a
471-line artifact rather than in a subset of it.

\paragraph{Stage 3 --- Close reading and decomposition.}
The module was read in full and decomposed into content blocks aligned to
objectives O1--O3. The decomposition is deliberately shallow and enumerative:
it records which concepts the note covers and how they are partitioned, without
re-deriving the underlying security content, which is the note's
responsibility and not this audit's.

\paragraph{Stage 4 --- Defect audit.}
Two classes of defect were checked for. The first is \emph{internal
contradiction}: a claim asserted in one part of the note that cannot be
reconciled with a claim asserted elsewhere in the same note. The second is
\emph{structural malformation}: a comprehension-check item whose answer key is
degenerate, for example because two options are textually identical and no
unique correct answer can exist. Both checks are properties of the note in
isolation and require no external ground truth, which keeps the audit
independent of any third-party source.

\paragraph{Stage 5 --- Drill protocol.}
The session concluded with the learner electing a drill protocol rather than a
further reading pass. Under this protocol the learner supplies examination
questions; the assistant answers each question, assigns a correctness score to
its own answer, and cites the supporting lines of the source note. The protocol
converts the audited note from a passive reading surface into an active
retrieval surface, and the line-level citation requirement makes each answer
falsifiable against the artifact rather than merely plausible.

\subsection{Access control schemes}

Five access control schemes are covered and are ranked by restrictiveness,
from least to most restrictive: discretionary access control (DAC),
mandatory access control (MAC), role-based access control (RBAC),
rule-based access control, and attribute-based access control (ABAC). The
ranking is a single total order; the note does not treat MAC and DAC as
equally restrictive, nor RBAC and rule-based as interchangeable.

The two defects differ in their consequence. The malformed review question is
self-announcing: a learner attempting it discovers the defect immediately, and
the cost is a wasted item. The password-expiry contradiction is the more
dangerous of the two, because it is a genuine internal inconsistency in a
normative statement---a practice described as common in industry alongside a
recommendation against that practice---and a learner who reads only one of the
two passages can absorb either position as the module's advice. Because the
note is a derived artifact, the contradiction may originate in the extraction
step rather than in the source PDF; distinguishing those two causes would
require re-reading the PDF, which was not part of this case study.

The session ended with the adoption of the drill protocol of

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Three observations follow from the case study, all of them descriptive.

\paragraph{Retrieval duality is a quality problem, not only a convenience
problem.} The two forms of the retrieved title are not redundant. A 12.2\,MB
PDF is a poor revision surface; a note tree is a poor provenance surface. Any
vault-scale study system must decide, per artifact, which form is
authoritative, and this decision is not recorded anywhere in the vault itself.
The case study's resolution---note tree as study surface, PDF as provenance
reference---is a reasonable default but is a convention adopted here, not a
property discovered in the material.

\paragraph{Objective alignment is auditable at the note level.} Because the
module states its three objectives explicitly and because its content
decomposes cleanly onto them, a completeness check is possible: every content
block in Table~\ref{tab:taxonomy} maps to O1, O2, or O3, and the passkey and
biometric blocks together show that O1 is the load-bearing objective. A note
that omits its stated objectives cannot be audited this way, which is an
argument for requiring objectives in extracted notes generally.

\paragraph{Internal contradiction is the dominant defect class.} Only one of
the two defects found was structural, and the structural one is self-
announcing. The substantive defect was a semantic contradiction between two
normative statements, which is precisely the class that survives into a
learner's belief system undetected. A keyword-level or rendering-level
extraction check would catch the malformed question; nothing short of a
semantic pass catches the expiry contradiction. This is the strongest
argument in the case study for spending audit effort on reasoning coherence
rather than on formatting.

\paragraph{Limitations.} Four limitations bound these findings. The study
covers one artifact from one vault on one machine. The module was audited
descriptively; no learner was tested against it, so nothing here speaks to
whether the module teaches effectively. The source PDF was not read, so the
audit cannot distinguish note-internal errors from extraction-introduced ones.
And the taxonomy in Table~\ref{tab:taxonomy} records coverage, not correctness:
a block being present in the note is not evidence that the note states it
correctly.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

A case-insensitive recursive search of a personal Obsidian vault resolved one
CompTIA study title into two artifacts, a 12.2\,MB source PDF and a
fifteen-module extracted note tree, and the note tree was selected as the
study surface. Module 7, ``Identity and Access Management,'' aligned to
SY0-701, was read in full at 471 lines and 21.6\,KB and found to cover
authentication credential types, authentication best practices, and access
control schemes across thirteen content blocks spanning the seven elements of
authenticity, password architecture and an eight-class attack taxonomy,
multifactor token types, hardware tokens, biometric modalities including
keystroke dynamics, digest protection with bcrypt, PBKDF2, and Argon2, the
single sign-on technology set, passkeys, and five rank-ordered access control
schemes. The quality audit found two defects: an unreconciled contradiction on
password expiry and a review question with two textually identical options.
The session concluded with a drill protocol in which learner-supplied
examination questions are answered with a correctness score and a line-level
citation into the source note. Extending the audit by reading the source PDF
against the note, and by line-resolving both defect sites, are the immediate
next steps.
