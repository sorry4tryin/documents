# Beginner Trading Knowledge System

A learning laboratory for understanding markets — not a signal service.

This document is a structured curriculum that takes a complete beginner from platform literacy through observation, hypothesis, simulation, testing, and review. Its purpose is **education, experimentation, and the development of understanding** — not immediate profit maximization.

The document teaches you **how to think about trading**: how to observe a market, separate fact from interpretation, form testable hypotheses, collect evidence, identify errors, and update your model.

## The Learner's Conceptual Model

This system uses a personal conceptual model for thinking about development. **It is a thinking tool, not an established scientific law.**

Growth is modeled as increasing exponentially with accumulated learning from school and related intellectual development:

$$
G(S) = ae^{kS}
$$

where $G$ is growth, $S$ is accumulated learning, and $a$, $k$ are constants of the model.

Understanding is modeled as increasing linearly with growth:

$$
U(G) = bG + c
$$

where $U$ is understanding and $b$, $c$ are constants of the model.

> These equations are the learner's conceptual model for thinking about development. They are not scientific laws and make no quantitative predictions.

## Priorities

1. **School** — the fixed priority. When academic work requires action, it comes first.
2. **Trading** — an optional priority. It is pursued only when school does not require action **and** a legitimate trading objective exists.
3. **Personal work** — outside this system.

> If there is no legitimate trading objective, no trading activity should be manufactured simply to fill time.

## The Curriculum Path

$$
\boxed{
\text{Complete Beginner}
\rightarrow
\text{Platform Literacy}
\rightarrow
\text{Market Literacy}
\rightarrow
\text{Observation}
\rightarrow
\text{Hypothesis}
\rightarrow
\text{Simulation}
\rightarrow
\text{Testing}
\rightarrow
\text{Review}
}
$$

Do not skip stages.

# Section 1 — Operating Model

## Purpose of the System

The system exists to develop the ability to **investigate markets independently**. It is a learning laboratory: you run small experiments, collect evidence, and update your understanding. It is not a place to receive signals, tips, or "what to buy."

## Definitions

### School
The fixed priority. Academic work that requires action comes before trading activity. School is also a source of learning ($S$) that feeds the growth model.

### Trading
An optional priority, pursued only when school does not require action and a legitimate objective exists. In this system, "trading" means *studying and experimenting with markets* — not necessarily placing real trades.

### Personal work
Everything outside school and trading. It remains outside this system.

### Growth
Increasing capability and knowledge over time. Modeled as $G(S) = ae^{kS}$ — the learner's conceptual model, not a law.

### Understanding
The usable clarity that results from growth. Modeled as $U(G) = bG + c$.

### Objective
A specific, observable outcome that a session is designed to produce. "Learn trading" is not an objective; "identify 10 higher-high structures on BTC" is.

### Session
An allocated period of time with a predefined objective, a completion condition, and an observable output. See [[#Section 2 — Trading Session System|Section 2]].

### Observation
Recording what the market does, without explaining it. Observation comes before interpretation.

### Hypothesis
A testable statement of the form "X may occur under conditions Y." Not a prediction.

### Experiment
A structured test of a hypothesis, with predefined conditions and a recorded result.

### Simulation
Practicing the full process — thesis, entry, invalidation, exit, review — without real money.

### Review
Examining the evidence produced, identifying errors, and updating the model.

## The Relationship Chain

```text
School
   ↓
Knowledge
   ↓
Growth
   ↓
Understanding
   ↓
Trading Investigation
   ↓
Experimentation
   ↓
Evidence
   ↓
Better Understanding
```

## Time Is Not Learning

**Time spent is not equivalent to learning.**

A two-hour session with no defined objective may produce less learning than a 30-minute session with a clear experiment. The unit of learning is the *observable output*, not the clock.

# Section 2 — Trading Session System

> A trading session is **an allocated period of time with a predefined objective, a completion condition, and an observable output.**

Every session must contain:

```markdown
## Session

**Objective:**

**Question:**

**Time allocation:**

**Method:**

**Completion condition:**

**Output:**

**What I learned:**

**What I still don't understand:**
```

A session ends when either:

1. The objective is completed, or
2. The allocated time expires.

Do not artificially extend sessions. If the time expires before the objective is complete, record what was learned and what remains — that is a valid output.

# Section 3 — Anti-Procrastination Rules

These rules exist to prevent **performative productivity** — activity that looks like work but produces no learning.

- "Learn trading" is not an objective.
- "Research crypto" is not an objective.
- "Watch trading videos" is not automatically productive.
- "Organize notes" does not count as trading research unless organization itself is the objective.
- A session must produce an observable output.
- Do not search for a new strategy merely because the current task is difficult.
- Do not change tools merely because progress feels slow.
- Do not manufacture trading activity when no legitimate objective exists.

The core distinction:

$$
\text{Activity} \neq \text{Progress}
$$

$$
\text{Progress} = \text{Evidence of increased capability or understanding}
$$

> If you cannot name the evidence a session produced, the session did not produce progress — regardless of how long it lasted.---

# Section 4 — Stage 1: Axiom Platform Literacy

Before attempting any live trading, learn to understand the environment. This stage is about **platform literacy**: knowing what the pieces are, what they do, and why they matter.

**Do not encourage live trading at this stage.** All exercises here are reading, exploring, and explaining.

For each concept use this structure:

```markdown
### [[Concept]]

**Definition:**

**What it does:**

**Why it matters:**

**What it looks like in Axiom:**

**Beginner misconception:**

**Question to investigate:**

**Practical exercise:**
```

## The Concepts

### [[Wallet]]

**Definition:** The software or interface that holds the cryptographic keys controlling access to your assets on a network.

**What it does:** Lets you view balances, send assets, and receive assets. The wallet holds *keys*, not the assets themselves.

**Why it matters:** If you lose access to the keys, you lose access to the assets. Understanding custody starts here.

**What it looks like in Axiom:** The account or wallet view shows your balances and transaction history. Deposits and withdrawals move assets between your Axiom wallet and external wallets.

**Beginner misconception:** "My coins are stored inside the wallet." The assets live on the network; the wallet holds the keys that control them.

**Question to investigate:** What happens to my assets if I lose access to my wallet?

**Practical exercise:** Locate the wallet/account view in Axiom and list every piece of information it displays.

### [[Balance]]

**Definition:** The amount of an asset currently available in your account.

**What it does:** Shows what you can use to trade, deposit, or withdraw.

**Why it matters:** Balance is the basis for position sizing and risk calculations. You cannot risk what you do not have.

**What it looks like in Axiom:** A balance figure next to each asset in your account, sometimes split into available and in-use amounts.

**Beginner misconception:** "Balance equals profit." Balance is a snapshot of what you hold; profit is a change over time.

**Question to investigate:** What is the difference between balance and equity?

**Practical exercise:** Record your balance for one asset at the start and end of a session, and note what changed.

### [[Asset]]

**Definition:** A thing of value that can be held or traded — in this system, primarily Bitcoin and memecoins.

**What it does:** Defines what you are studying and what you can trade.

**Why it matters:** Different assets behave differently. The curriculum uses Bitcoin as the reference asset and memecoins as the contrast.

**What it looks like in Axiom:** A list of tradeable assets, each with a price, market cap, and volume display.

**Beginner misconception:** "All assets behave the same way." Bitcoin and memecoins differ enormously in volatility, liquidity, and what drives their price.

**Question to investigate:** What determines whether an asset is worth studying?

**Practical exercise:** List every asset available in Axiom and note which are coins and which are tokens.

### [[Token]]

**Definition:** A digital asset issued on an existing network, often representing a project or community. Most memecoins are tokens.

**What it does:** Enables assets to be created and traded on top of a network without their own blockchain.

**Why it matters:** Token mechanics — supply, distribution, contract — differ from a coin like Bitcoin and matter for risk.

**What it looks like in Axiom:** Tokens appear in the asset list, often with a network label and contract address.

**Beginner misconception:** "Token and coin are the same thing." A coin runs its own network; a token runs on someone else's.

**Question to investigate:** Which network does this token live on, and why does that matter?

**Practical exercise:** Pick one memecoin and identify its network and contract address.

### [[Trading Pair]]

**Definition:** Two assets traded against each other, such as BTC/USDT. One is the base asset, the other the quote asset.

**What it does:** Defines what you are buying and what you are paying with.

**Why it matters:** The pair determines the price you see and the asset you receive. Confusing base and quote is a common beginner error.

**What it looks like in Axiom:** A pair selector showing combinations like BTC/USDT, BTC/USDC, or a memecoin paired with a stablecoin.

**Beginner misconception:** "Buying BTC" without noticing which quote asset the pair uses.

**Question to investigate:** What changes if the quote asset of a pair changes?

**Practical exercise:** List three pairs and identify the base and quote asset of each.

### [[Price]]

**Definition:** The amount of quote asset required to buy one unit of the base asset at a given moment.

**What it does:** Summarizes the most recent agreement between buyers and sellers.

**Why it matters:** Price is the primary thing you observe. It is a record of what happened, not a promise of what will happen.

**What it looks like in Axiom:** A price figure on the chart and in the order book, updating continuously.

**Beginner misconception:** "Price equals value." Price is what someone last paid; value is a judgment.

**Question to investigate:** What makes price move?

**Practical exercise:** Record the BTC price at three different times in one day, with timestamps.

### [[Market Capitalization]]

**Definition:** Price multiplied by circulating supply. A rough measure of an asset's size.

**What it does:** Lets you compare assets of very different scales.

**Why it matters:** Market cap is a size indicator, not a safety indicator and not a liquidity indicator.

**What it looks like in Axiom:** A market cap figure in the asset list or asset detail view.

**Beginner misconception:** "High market cap means it is safe" or "high market cap means I can trade large amounts easily." Neither follows.

**Question to investigate:** Why does market capitalization not equal liquidity?

**Practical exercise:** Compare the market cap of BTC with one memecoin and write down what the difference does and does not tell you.

### [[Liquidity]]

**Definition:** How easily an asset can be bought or sold without moving its price significantly.

**What it does:** Determines how close your execution is to the price you expected.

**Why it matters:** Low liquidity means larger price impact, wider spreads, and more slippage. It is one of the most important differences between BTC and memecoins.

**What it looks like in Axiom:** Order book depth and the bid-ask spread for a pair.

**Beginner misconception:** "High volume means high liquidity." They are related but not identical; liquidity is about depth at the current price.

**Question to investigate:** How can I measure liquidity?

**Practical exercise:** Compare the order book depth of BTC with one memecoin at the same moment.

### [[Volume]]

**Definition:** The amount of an asset traded within a period of time.

**What it does:** Shows how much activity is happening in a market.

**Why it matters:** Volume is an indicator of attention and participation. It tells you *that* something is happening, not *why*.

**What it looks like in Axiom:** A volume figure per period, often shown as bars under the price chart.

**Beginner misconception:** "Volume predicts direction." Volume describes activity; direction is a separate question.

**Question to investigate:** Does volume increase before price moves, or after?

**Practical exercise:** Record BTC volume at three different times of day and note the differences.

### [[Order and Execution]]

**Definition:** An order is an instruction to buy or sell. Execution is the moment the order is filled.

**What it does:** Converts your intent into a trade. Market orders fill immediately at the best available price; limit orders fill only at your price or better.

**Why it matters:** The type of order you use changes the price you get and whether you get filled at all.

**What it looks like in Axiom:** An order entry panel with order type, amount, and price fields.

**Beginner misconception:** "Orders always fill at the price I see." Market orders can fill worse than displayed; limit orders may never fill.

**Question to investigate:** What is the difference between a market order and a limit order?

**Practical exercise:** In simulation mode, place one market order and one limit order, and record what happened.

### [[Slippage]]

**Definition:** The difference between the price you expected and the price at which your order actually executed.

**What it does:** Adds a hidden cost to trades, especially large orders in thin markets.

**Why it matters:** Slippage can turn a profitable idea into a losing one. It is largest where liquidity is lowest — which is exactly where memecoins live.

**What it looks like in Axiom:** Your fill price differing from the displayed price, most visibly on large or fast orders.

**Beginner misconception:** "Slippage is a fee charged by the platform." It is a market effect, not a fee.

**Question to investigate:** What conditions increase slippage?

**Practical exercise:** Explain slippage using three hypothetical transactions of increasing size in a low-liquidity pair.

### [[Fees]]

**Definition:** The costs charged for executing trades on the platform.

**What it does:** Reduces the net result of every trade, win or lose.

**Why it matters:** Fees compound. A strategy must overcome fees before it produces any net result.

**What it looks like in Axiom:** A fee schedule and a fee line on each executed trade.

**Beginner misconception:** "Fees are negligible." Over many trades, fees are a real and predictable cost.

**Question to investigate:** How do fees affect the result of a strategy?

**Practical exercise:** Calculate the fees on five hypothetical trades using the platform's fee schedule.

### [[Position]]

**Definition:** Your current exposure to an asset — what you hold, in what size, and in which direction.

**What it does:** Defines your risk. Until you have a position, you have no market risk.

**Why it matters:** All risk calculations start from the position: size, entry, and invalidation.

**What it looks like in Axiom:** A positions panel listing open positions with size and entry price.

**Beginner misconception:** "Position equals profit." A position is exposure; profit is only realized on exit.

**Question to investigate:** What is my exposure right now, and what is its maximum possible loss?

**Practical exercise:** Describe a hypothetical position — asset, size, entry — and compute its maximum loss.

### [[Entry]]

**Definition:** The price and conditions at which you open a position.

**What it does:** Marks the starting point of your risk calculation.

**Why it matters:** Entry defines your reference for invalidation and reward. A plan that cannot state its entry cannot be tested.

**What it looks like in Axiom:** The price at which your opening order fills, recorded on the position.

**Beginner misconception:** "Entry must be perfect." Entry only needs to be predefined and consistent.

**Question to investigate:** What makes an entry rule testable?

**Practical exercise:** Write an entry rule for a hypothetical trade before looking at any chart.

### [[Exit]]

**Definition:** The price and conditions at which you close a position.

**What it does:** Realizes the outcome of the trade.

**Why it matters:** The exit is where the result happens. Deciding it after entry invites emotional decisions.

**What it looks like in Axiom:** Exit orders and the fill price when you close a position.

**Beginner misconception:** "I will decide when to exit when the time comes." That is how small losses become large ones.

**Question to investigate:** What happens to a trade that has no exit plan?

**Practical exercise:** Write an exit plan for a hypothetical trade before entry, including an invalidation price.

### [[Transaction]]

**Definition:** A recorded transfer or execution on the network or platform.

**What it does:** Creates the permanent record of what happened — when, what, how much, at what price.

**Why it matters:** Transactions are the raw evidence of market activity. Learning to read them is learning to read what actually happened.

**What it looks like in Axiom:** A transaction history with timestamps, assets, amounts, and prices.

**Beginner misconception:** "A transaction is the same as a profit or loss." A transaction is a record; profit is a comparison over time.

**Question to investigate:** What information does a transaction record contain?

**Practical exercise:** Inspect one transaction record and list every field it contains.

### [[Network]]

**Definition:** The underlying system — the blockchain — that records and settles transactions for an asset.

**What it does:** Determines how fast and how expensive transfers are, and where an asset actually lives.

**Why it matters:** Assets exist on networks, not on exchanges. Choosing the wrong network for a transfer can lose funds.

**What it looks like in Axiom:** A network selector when depositing or withdrawing, and a network label on each asset.

**Beginner misconception:** "All networks are the same." Networks differ in speed, cost, and which assets they support.

**Question to investigate:** Why does network choice matter for a transfer?

**Practical exercise:** Identify which network a memecoin uses and what that network is for.

### [[Risk]]

**Definition:** The possibility of losing value — the uncertainty of outcomes.

**What it does:** Defines what trading actually is: making decisions under uncertainty.

**Why it matters:** Everything in this curriculum — position size, invalidation, liquidity, slippage — is a way of understanding and limiting risk.

**What it looks like in Axiom:** Risk appears in position size, price impact, volatility, and the possibility of loss on every trade.

**Beginner misconception:** "Risk is how much I could win." Risk is what you can lose.

**Question to investigate:** What is my maximum possible loss on a given trade?

**Practical exercise:** Compute the maximum possible loss of a hypothetical position, including fees and slippage.

### [[Custody]]

**Definition:** Who holds and controls the assets and the keys that access them.

**What it does:** Determines who can move the assets and what happens if a platform fails.

**Why it matters:** Exchange custody (the platform holds your assets) differs from self-custody (you hold your own keys). Each has different risks.

**What it looks like in Axiom:** Your assets are held in custody by the platform while in your account.

**Beginner misconception:** "Not your keys, not your coins" is a slogan, not a technical fact. It describes a real custody difference.

**Question to investigate:** What happens to my assets if the platform fails?

**Practical exercise:** Research what custody means on Axiom and write a one-paragraph explanation.

## Completion Criterion

> The learner can explain the major components of the platform without guessing.---

# Section 5 — Stage 2: Bitcoin Fundamentals

Bitcoin is the reference asset for this curriculum. It is the largest, most liquid, and most studied cryptocurrency — the best place to learn market mechanics before studying memecoins.

For each concept provide:

1. Definition
2. Simple explanation
3. Historical/function explanation
4. Example
5. Observation exercise
6. Question that leads to the next concept

Throughout this stage, repeatedly ask:

> "What am I actually observing?"

rather than:

> "What do I think the price will do?"

## The Concepts

### BTC

1. **Definition:** Bitcoin, ticker BTC — the first and largest cryptocurrency.
2. **Simple explanation:** Digital money that exists on its own network, with a fixed supply.
3. **Historical/function explanation:** Created in 2009 as a decentralized system that records transactions without a central authority. It is the reference asset against which most of crypto is measured.
4. **Example:** The BTC/USDT pair on Axiom.
5. **Observation exercise:** Record the BTC price once daily for one week. Numbers only, no commentary.
6. **Question that leads to the next concept:** What limits how much BTC can ever exist?

### Supply

1. **Definition:** The total amount of BTC that exists or will ever exist.
2. **Simple explanation:** There is a hard cap of 21 million BTC.
3. **Historical/function explanation:** The cap is written into the network's rules. New BTC is created at a decreasing rate over time.
4. **Example:** Circulating supply is the amount already created; the cap is 21 million.
5. **Observation exercise:** Look up BTC's current circulating supply and check how close it is to the cap.
6. **Question that leads to the next concept:** How does supply combine with price to describe the size of an asset?

### Market Capitalization

1. **Definition:** Price multiplied by circulating supply.
2. **Simple explanation:** A rough measure of how big an asset is.
3. **Historical/function explanation:** Used across finance to compare assets of different scales. It is a size measure, not a value judgment.
4. **Example:** BTC's market cap is orders of magnitude larger than any memecoin's.
5. **Observation exercise:** Compute BTC's market cap from its price and circulating supply, then compare it with one memecoin.
6. **Question that leads to the next concept:** Does a large market cap mean you can trade large amounts easily?

### Liquidity

1. **Definition:** How easily an asset can be bought or sold without moving its price significantly.
2. **Simple explanation:** How deep the market is at the current price.
3. **Historical/function explanation:** BTC is the most liquid cryptocurrency because of its size and the number of participants. Liquidity is about depth, not just size.
4. **Example:** A large BTC order moves the price less than the same order in a small memecoin.
5. **Observation exercise:** Compare the bid-ask spread and order book depth of BTC with one memecoin at the same moment.
6. **Question that leads to the next concept:** How do I see how much activity is happening in a market?

### Volume

1. **Definition:** The amount of an asset traded within a period of time.
2. **Simple explanation:** How much is changing hands.
3. **Historical/function explanation:** Volume spikes around events and periods of attention. It measures activity, not direction.
4. **Example:** BTC's daily volume figure on Axiom.
5. **Observation exercise:** Record BTC volume at three different times of day for several days and note the pattern.
6. **Question that leads to the next concept:** How much does price move in a given period?

### Volatility

1. **Definition:** The magnitude of price fluctuation over time.
2. **Simple explanation:** How wild the price is.
3. **Historical/function explanation:** BTC is volatile compared with fiat currencies but far less volatile than most memecoins. Volatility is measurable, not a feeling.
4. **Example:** The daily percentage range of BTC versus a memecoin on the same day.
5. **Observation exercise:** Compute BTC's daily high-low range for five days and average it.
6. **Question that leads to the next concept:** What exactly is the price I am watching?

### Price

1. **Definition:** The amount of quote asset agreed for one unit of the base asset at a given moment.
2. **Simple explanation:** The last agreement between a buyer and a seller.
3. **Historical/function explanation:** Price is set by matching orders in an order book. It is a record of what happened, not a statement about the future.
4. **Example:** BTC trading at a specific USDT value on the chart.
5. **Observation exercise:** Watch the price for 15 minutes and write down only what happened — no predictions.
6. **Question that leads to the next concept:** Do price movements form patterns over time?

### Market Structure

1. **Definition:** The pattern of highs and lows that price makes over time.
2. **Simple explanation:** The shape of the chart.
3. **Historical/function explanation:** Traders describe structure — sequences of highs and lows — to organize observation into a shared language.
4. **Example:** A sequence of higher highs and higher lows on the daily chart.
5. **Observation exercise:** Mark the last 20 significant highs and lows on a BTC chart without adding any opinion.
6. **Question that leads to the next concept:** Does the structure look the same on every timeframe?

### Timeframes

1. **Definition:** The interval each candle represents — 1 minute, 1 hour, 1 day, and so on.
2. **Simple explanation:** The zoom level of the chart.
3. **Historical/function explanation:** Different timeframes show different detail. The same period looks different at different zoom levels.
4. **Example:** The same week of BTC shown as 1-hour candles and as 1-day candles.
5. **Observation exercise:** Compare the same period of BTC on two timeframes and note what each reveals or hides.
6. **Question that leads to the next concept:** What information does a single candle contain?

### Candlesticks

1. **Definition:** A bar that shows the open, high, low, and close of a period.
2. **Simple explanation:** A summary of what price did in that period.
3. **Historical/function explanation:** Candle charts come from Japanese rice trading in the 18th century and remain the standard way to display price.
4. **Example:** A green candle means close above open; a red candle means close below open. The wicks show the extremes.
5. **Observation exercise:** Describe five candles in plain words — open, high, low, close, range — without interpreting them.
6. **Question that leads to the next concept:** Do sequences of candles form recognizable directions?

### Trends

1. **Definition:** A sustained directional movement in price — up, down, or sideways.
2. **Simple explanation:** The market's current direction.
3. **Historical/function explanation:** Trend-following is one of the oldest trading ideas. An uptrend is defined by structure, not by opinion.
4. **Example:** An uptrend as a sequence of higher highs and higher lows.
5. **Observation exercise:** Identify the current trend of BTC on the daily chart using only structure.
6. **Question that leads to the next concept:** What does the market look like when there is no trend?

### Ranges

1. **Definition:** Price oscillating between a support level and a resistance level.
2. **Simple explanation:** Sideways movement.
3. **Historical/function explanation:** Markets alternate between trending and ranging. Recognizing which state you are in changes what you expect.
4. **Example:** BTC bouncing between two horizontal levels for several weeks.
5. **Observation exercise:** Find a range on a BTC chart and mark its top and bottom.
6. **Question that leads to the next concept:** What holds price up at the bottom of a range?

### Support

1. **Definition:** A level where price has repeatedly stopped falling.
2. **Simple explanation:** A floor.
3. **Historical/function explanation:** Support exists because, at that level, buyers have historically stepped in. It is an observed pattern, not a law.
4. **Example:** Price bouncing off the same level three times.
5. **Observation exercise:** Mark every support level you can identify on a BTC chart and count how many times each held.
6. **Question that leads to the next concept:** What stops price from rising?

### Resistance

1. **Definition:** A level where price has repeatedly stopped rising.
2. **Simple explanation:** A ceiling.
3. **Historical/function explanation:** Resistance is where sellers have historically stepped in. Like support, it is an observed pattern.
4. **Example:** Price being rejected at the same level twice.
5. **Observation exercise:** Mark every resistance level on a BTC chart and note what happened at each touch.
6. **Question that leads to the next concept:** What happens when price moves through a level?

### Breakouts

1. **Definition:** Price moving through a level — usually resistance — with conviction.
2. **Simple explanation:** The ceiling breaks.
3. **Historical/function explanation:** A breakout is only meaningful if defined in advance: which level, which timeframe, what counts as "through."
4. **Example:** Price closing above a resistance level that held for weeks.
5. **Observation exercise:** Find a breakout on a BTC chart and record the volume at the moment of the break.
6. **Question that leads to the next concept:** What about when price breaks downward?

### Breakdowns

1. **Definition:** Price moving through a level — usually support — downward.
2. **Simple explanation:** The floor breaks.
3. **Historical/function explanation:** Breakdowns are the mirror of breakouts. The same rules of definition apply.
4. **Example:** Price closing below a support level that held for weeks.
5. **Observation exercise:** Find a breakdown on a BTC chart and record what happened in the following candles.
6. **Question that leads to the next concept:** How do I describe structure precisely?

### Higher Highs

1. **Definition:** Each peak higher than the previous peak.
2. **Simple explanation:** Price keeps making new highs.
3. **Historical/function explanation:** Higher highs are one half of the definition of an uptrend.
4. **Example:** A chart where each swing high is above the last.
5. **Observation exercise:** Find and mark three consecutive higher highs on a BTC chart.
6. **Question that leads to the next concept:** What confirms that an uptrend is real?

### Higher Lows

1. **Definition:** Each trough higher than the previous trough.
2. **Simple explanation:** Buyers are stepping in at higher levels.
3. **Historical/function explanation:** Higher lows are the other half of the uptrend definition. Together with higher highs, they describe an uptrend.
4. **Example:** A chart where each swing low is above the last.
5. **Observation exercise:** Find and mark three consecutive higher lows on a BTC chart.
6. **Question that leads to the next concept:** What does a downtrend look like in this language?

### Lower Highs

1. **Definition:** Each peak lower than the previous peak.
2. **Simple explanation:** Sellers are stepping in at lower levels.
3. **Historical/function explanation:** Lower highs are one half of the definition of a downtrend.
4. **Example:** A chart where each swing high is below the last.
5. **Observation exercise:** Find and mark three consecutive lower highs on a BTC chart.
6. **Question that leads to the next concept:** What completes the picture of a downtrend?

### Lower Lows

1. **Definition:** Each trough lower than the previous trough.
2. **Simple explanation:** Price keeps making new lows.
3. **Historical/function explanation:** Lower lows complete the downtrend definition alongside lower highs.
4. **Example:** A chart where each swing low is below the last.
5. **Observation exercise:** Describe BTC's current structure using only the terms higher highs, higher lows, lower highs, and lower lows.
6. **Question that leads to the next concept:** How do I record all of this systematically?---

# Section 6 — Stage 3: Memecoin Fundamentals

Memecoins are tokens driven primarily by narrative, attention, and community rather than by a product or utility. They differ from Bitcoin in almost every dimension that matters for study: volatility, liquidity, supply mechanics, and what moves their price.

The purpose of this stage is to understand **how memecoins work as objects of study** — not to find one to buy.

## The Concepts

### Narrative
**What it is:** A shared story, theme, event, or cultural idea that attracts attention toward an asset.
**Why it matters for memecoins:** Narrative is often the primary driver of memecoin activity. Price can move because a story spread, not because anything about the token changed.
**How it differs from Bitcoin:** Bitcoin has a long, stable narrative; memecoins have short, rapidly changing ones.

### Attention
**What it is:** The amount of interest an asset receives from the public — visible in volume, social activity, and search interest.
**Why it matters for memecoins:** Attention is the fuel of memecoin markets. It can appear quickly and disappear faster.
**How it differs from Bitcoin:** Bitcoin has persistent baseline attention; memecoins often have spikes.

### Community
**What it is:** The group of people who hold, promote, and discuss a token.
**Why it matters for memecoins:** A strong community can sustain attention and liquidity; a weak one leaves a token dependent on outside interest.
**How it differs from Bitcoin:** Bitcoin's community is broad and institutional; memecoin communities are often small, concentrated, and highly active.

### Liquidity
**What it is:** How easily the token can be bought or sold without moving price.
**Why it matters for memecoins:** Memecoin liquidity is often thin and can be withdrawn. Thin liquidity means large price swings and heavy slippage.
**How it differs from Bitcoin:** BTC is the most liquid crypto asset; most memecoins are among the least.

### Market Capitalization
**What it is:** Price multiplied by circulating supply.
**Why it matters for memecoins:** A small market cap can grow or shrink dramatically. Market cap says nothing about how easy the token is to trade.
**How it differs from Bitcoin:** BTC's market cap is enormous and relatively stable; memecoin market caps are small and erratic.

### Volume
**What it is:** Amount of the token traded in a period.
**Why it matters for memecoins:** Volume is the visible trace of attention. High volume can exist with thin liquidity if the same tokens trade repeatedly.
**How it differs from Bitcoin:** BTC volume is deep and continuous; memecoin volume is spiky and event-driven.

### Volatility
**What it is:** The magnitude of price fluctuation.
**Why it matters for memecoins:** Memecoins can move tens of percent in hours. This is not an opportunity; it is a risk property to study.
**How it differs from Bitcoin:** BTC is volatile; memecoins are far more so.

### Token Supply
**What it is:** The number of tokens that exist, and whether that number can change.
**Why it matters for memecoins:** Supply can be fixed, inflationary, or burnable. Supply mechanics affect how price responds to demand.
**How it differs from Bitcoin:** BTC's supply is capped and predictable; memecoin supply rules vary wildly.

### Distribution
**What it is:** How tokens are spread among holders.
**Why it matters for memecoins:** Concentrated distribution means a few holders can move the market.
**How it differs from Bitcoin:** BTC distribution is broad; memecoins are often concentrated at launch.

### Holder Concentration
**What it is:** The share of supply held by the largest wallets.
**Why it matters for memecoins:** High concentration means large sell pressure is possible from few actors.
**How it differs from Bitcoin:** Concentration is a risk question for any asset, but it is acute for memecoins.

### Token Age
**What it is:** How long the token has existed.
**Why it matters for memecoins:** New tokens have no track record and often no established liquidity.
**How it differs from Bitcoin:** BTC has years of history; most memecoins have weeks or months.

### Launch Conditions
**What it is:** How and when the token was created and first distributed.
**Why it matters for memecoins:** Launch mechanics — who got tokens, at what price, with what lockups — shape everything that follows.
**How it differs from Bitcoin:** BTC was mined openly; memecoin launches are often controlled by a small group.

### Liquidity Conditions
**What it is:** Where and how much liquidity exists for the token, and whether it is locked or removable.
**Why it matters for memecoins:** Removable liquidity is a rug-pull vector. Locked liquidity reduces that specific risk.
**How it differs from Bitcoin:** BTC's liquidity is spread across many venues; memecoin liquidity is often a single pool.

### Speculation
**What it is:** Buying based on expected price movement rather than underlying value.
**Why it matters for memecoins:** Memecoin markets are almost entirely speculative. That is a fact to study, not a reason to participate.
**How it differs from Bitcoin:** Both are speculative, but memecoins have no non-speculative foundation to fall back on.

### Manipulation Risks
**What it is:** The possibility that price is influenced by actors with concentrated holdings or information.
**Why it matters for memecoins:** Small markets are easier to move. Observed price behavior may reflect manipulation rather than genuine demand.
**How it differs from Bitcoin:** Manipulation is possible anywhere but is far easier in thin memecoin markets.

### Rug-Pull Risks
**What it is:** The risk that developers or large holders remove liquidity or dump their holdings, collapsing the price.
**Why it matters for memecoins:** This is a structural risk of small tokens, not a rare accident.
**How it differs from Bitcoin:** Bitcoin has no developer with that power; memecoins often do.

### Contract and Security Considerations
**What it is:** The token's smart contract code and its security properties.
**Why it matters for memecoins:** Contracts can contain functions that mint, freeze, or steal. Reading a contract is a skill; not reading one is a risk.
**How it differs from Bitcoin:** Bitcoin's rules are fixed and battle-tested; memecoin contracts are new and unaudited.

## Observation vs. Interpretation vs. Hypothesis vs. Prediction

```text
Observation
vs.
Interpretation
vs.
Hypothesis
vs.
Prediction
```

- **Observation:** What happened. "Volume increased by 300% over six hours." Fact, timestamped, verifiable.
- **Interpretation:** What you think it means. "The increase was caused by a viral post." A claim that may or may not be true.
- **Hypothesis:** A testable statement. "If a viral post caused the volume increase, then volume should have risen shortly after the post appeared." Can be checked.
- **Prediction:** A statement about the future. "The price will keep rising." Not a test — a hope.

> Do not present speculative relationships as guaranteed causal mechanisms. A narrative that *coincides* with a price move is not proof that it *caused* the move.

# Section 8 — Stage 5: Narrative Research

> A narrative is **a shared story, theme, event, or cultural idea that attracts attention toward an asset or group of assets.**

Narratives are the primary driver of memecoin markets. This stage teaches you to investigate them — to trace how a story moves through attention, volume, and price — without assuming the chain is causal.

## The Research Chain

```text
Event
↓
Attention
↓
Narrative
↓
Asset discovery
↓
Volume
↓
Liquidity
↓
Price behavior
↓
Market reaction
```

**Do not assume this chain is causal.** The chain is a map of what to look at, not a claim about how markets work. Your job is to investigate whether the observed relationship actually exists.

## Research Questions

- What caused attention to increase?
- When did attention begin?
- Did volume increase before price?
- Did price increase before attention?
- Did liquidity change?
- Did the narrative spread across multiple assets?
- How long did attention persist?
- What happened when attention disappeared?

## How to Investigate

1. **Timestamp everything.** Record when the event happened, when attention rose, when volume changed, when price moved.
2. **Separate sources.** Social posts, news, and price data are different evidence. Note which source each claim comes from.
3. **Compare assets.** Did the same narrative move multiple tokens, or only one?
4. **Look for the absence.** What happened to price and volume *after* attention faded? That is evidence too.
5. **Record uncertainty.** If you cannot verify a step in the chain, write that you cannot verify it.

A narrative note is complete when it states what happened, when it happened, what evidence supports each step, and what remains uncertain.---

# Section 9 — Stage 6: Hypothesis Formation

Observation produces facts. Hypotheses convert those facts into something testable.

$$
\boxed{\text{Observation} \rightarrow \text{Hypothesis} \rightarrow \text{Test}}
$$

Use this template:

```markdown
## Hypothesis

**Observation:**

**Hypothesis:**

**Conditions required:**

**Expected behavior:**

**Invalidation condition:**

**Test method:**

**Sample size:**

**Result:**

**Conclusion:**

**Remaining uncertainty:**
```

## A Hypothesis Is Not a Prediction

A prediction says: "X is going to happen."

A hypothesis says: "I think X may occur under conditions Y, so I will test it."

The difference matters. A prediction is a claim about the future that cannot be examined. A hypothesis is a statement with conditions, an expected behavior, and an invalidation condition — it can be tested, and it can be wrong.

A good hypothesis:

- Follows from a specific observation.
- States the conditions under which it applies.
- States what behavior would confirm it.
- States what behavior would invalidate it.
- Can be tested with a defined method and sample size.

If any of these is missing, the hypothesis is not ready to test.

# Section 10 — Stage 7: Backtesting

Backtesting is the process of testing a **defined rule** against **historical market data**. It is how you collect evidence about an idea before risking anything on it.

## Key Concepts

| Concept | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Sample | One instance of the setup being tested. |
| Variable | Something that changes between samples — price, volume, timeframe, conditions. |
| Condition | A predefined requirement that must be true for the setup to apply. |
| Entry | The predefined rule for opening the trade. |
| Exit | The predefined rule for closing the trade. |
| Stop / invalidation | The predefined price or condition that ends the trade as a loss. |
| Risk | What you lose when the trade is invalidated. |
| Reward | What you gain when the trade works. |
| Win rate | The fraction of samples that ended in profit. |
| Expectancy | Average outcome per trade across the sample. |
| Drawdown | The decline from peak results during testing. |
| Bias | Ways the test is not neutral — choosing samples after seeing the outcome, changing rules mid-test. |
| Overfitting | Making rules fit the historical data so precisely that they describe noise, not behavior. |
| Sample size | The number of samples tested. Small samples cannot support strong conclusions. |

## Beginner Backtesting Exercises

1. **The 20-example test.** Find 20 historical examples of a specific setup. Record whether the predefined conditions were satisfied and what happened afterward. Do not change the conditions while collecting.
2. **The invalidation test.** Take one setup and record, for each sample, whether the invalidation was hit before the target. Count how often the rule worked as defined.
3. **The bias check.** After a test, list every way the test could have been biased — samples chosen after the fact, rules adjusted mid-test, unclear conditions.
4. **The sample-size test.** Run the same test on 10 samples and on 30 samples. Note how the result changes.

## Rules of Backtesting

- Define the rule **before** looking at the data.
- Record every sample, including the failures.
- Do not fabricate performance statistics.
- Do not imply that historical performance guarantees future performance.

> A backtest is evidence about the past under specific assumptions. It is not a promise.

# Section 12 — Stage 9: Risk

Risk management is the most important section in this document.

> Risk management is not a method for making an uncertain trade safe. It is a method for limiting the consequences of being wrong.

## The Concepts

### Capital Preservation
The first objective of any trading system. If the capital is gone, there is nothing left to learn with. Every other rule serves this one.

### Position Sizing
How much of your capital a single trade uses. Size is the main lever you control. A position sized so that its maximum loss is small relative to your capital is a survivable position.

### Risk per Trade
The amount you are willing to lose on one trade. A common approach is a fixed fraction of capital — for example, risking 1% per trade. On a balance of 1000 units, that is a maximum loss of 10 units per trade.

### Stop / Invalidation
The predefined price or condition that ends a trade as a loss. It is decided before entry, not after. A trade without an invalidation is not a defined trade.

### Maximum Loss
The worst-case outcome of a position, including fees and slippage. You should be able to compute this for every position before you take it.

### Liquidity Risk
The risk that you cannot exit at a reasonable price because the market is thin. It is highest in memecoins and grows with position size.

### Slippage
The difference between expected and actual execution price. It is a cost of thin liquidity and large orders, and it must be included in any loss calculation.

### Counterparty Risk
The risk that the platform or party on the other side of your trade fails or behaves badly. Exchange custody, withdrawal limits, and platform solvency all fall here.

### Smart-Contract Risk
The risk that the token's contract contains flaws or malicious functions. Relevant to any token, especially new memecoins.

### Scam Risk
The risk that the asset itself is a fraud — fake project, fake liquidity, fake volume. If something cannot be verified, it is a risk.

### Market Manipulation
The risk that price is being moved by actors with concentrated holdings. Observed price behavior may not reflect genuine demand.

### Emotional Decision-Making
The risk you bring yourself: fear, excitement, and the urge to act when there is no defined reason. The session system and predefined rules are the defense.

### Leverage
Borrowed exposure that multiplies both gains and losses. Leverage can turn a small adverse move into a total loss. For a beginner, leverage is not a tool; it is a risk multiplier.

## The Core Idea

Every risk concept in this section answers one question: **what is the worst thing that can happen, and have I limited it before it happens?**

Position size limits loss. Invalidation defines the loss. Liquidity and slippage determine whether the loss is what you expected. Counterparty, contract, and scam risk determine whether the asset and platform can be trusted at all.

None of this makes an uncertain trade safe. It makes being wrong survivable — which is what allows you to keep learning.---

The goal of this system is to make you able to investigate subjects independently. When a concept appears that you do not fully understand, run it through the recursive questioning framework:

## How to Use the Rabbit-Hole

- Run the chain whenever a concept appears that you cannot explain without guessing.
- Each answer becomes the input to the next question. The chain ends when you reach something you can **test**.
- If a question cannot be answered from what you have, that is a legitimate session objective for the next session.
- The chain is a path to a test, not an excuse to wander. If you are three levels deep with no test in sight, return to the surface.

# Section 18 — Progress Model

Progress is tracked by **capability**, not hours. The model tracks six capabilities:

$$
K = \text{knowledge}
$$

$$
O = \text{observation ability}
$$

$$
H = \text{hypothesis quality}
$$

$$
T = \text{testing ability}
$$

$$
R = \text{risk understanding}
$$

$$
E = \text{execution consistency}
$$

| Capability | What it measures | Evidence examples |
|---|---|---|
| $K$ — Knowledge | What you can explain without guessing | Definitions written in your own words |
| $O$ — Observation | How cleanly you separate fact from interpretation | Completed observation templates |
| $H$ — Hypothesis | How testable your hypotheses are | Hypotheses with conditions and invalidation |
| $T$ — Testing | How honestly you collect evidence | Backtest tables, recorded failures |
| $R$ — Risk | How well you limit consequences of being wrong | Risk rules, max-loss calculations |
| $E$ — Execution | How consistently you follow your own rules | Journal entries matching plans |

**Do not combine these into an arbitrary "trading score."** A single number hides everything that matters. Instead, assess each capability separately and note where evidence is strong, where it is weak, and where uncertainty remains.

Review the model weekly. A capability improves only when new evidence supports the improvement.

# Section 19 — Rules for Real-Money Transition

Real-money trading is not a stage in this curriculum to be rushed toward. It is a transition with objective prerequisites. Do not pass any of these by guessing.

- [ ] I understand the platform mechanics — wallet, balance, orders, execution, fees.
- [ ] I understand transaction costs — fees, slippage, spread.
- [ ] I understand slippage and can explain when it increases.
- [ ] I understand liquidity and can measure it for the assets I would trade.
- [ ] I have completed a defined number of simulated trades with complete journal entries.
- [ ] I maintain a trading journal with a predefined thesis for every trade.
- [ ] My strategy has predefined conditions — entry, invalidation, exit.
- [ ] I have demonstrated the ability to follow those conditions without deviation.
- [ ] I understand the maximum possible loss of any position I would take.
- [ ] I understand that past performance does not guarantee future results.

This document does not prescribe a specific dollar amount, does not promise profitability, and does not provide personalized financial advice. The decision to transition, and the amount involved, is yours alone — made with evidence, not enthusiasm.

# The Core Principle

$$
\boxed{
\text{Curiosity}
\rightarrow
\text{Question}
\rightarrow
\text{Observation}
\rightarrow
\text{Hypothesis}
\rightarrow
\text{Experiment}
\rightarrow
\text{Evidence}
\rightarrow
\text{Understanding}
\rightarrow
\text{Better Question}
}
$$

The goal is not to become someone who "knows how to trade."

The goal is to become someone who can:

1. Observe a market.
2. Describe what happened.
3. Separate fact from interpretation.
4. Form a testable hypothesis.
5. Collect evidence.
6. Identify errors.
7. Update their model.
8. Repeat the process independently.

This document is a **learning laboratory**, not a signal service. It teaches you how to investigate markets — it does not tell you what to buy.

Nothing in this document is a recommendation to buy, sell, long, short, or hold any specific asset.
