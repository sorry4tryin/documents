## 1 - The Due Process Voluntariness Approach

When a suspect makes an **involuntary** statement, that statement is not admissible to prove guilt.

- **Confession**: a person implicates themself in criminal activity *following* police questioning and/or interrogation
- **Admission**: need not be preceded by police questioning — a person can simply admit involvement without any police encouragement
- Throughout the chapter, confession and admission are treated synonymously

### Foundation: Brown v. Mississippi (297 U.S. 278 [1936])
- Illustrative case from the era when the Fifth and Sixth Amendments did **not** apply to the states
- Officers used whippings and other brutal methods to extract confessions from three African American defendants, who were convicted on those confessions alone
- Supreme Court analyzed the case under the **Fourteenth Amendment's due process clause** and invalidated the convictions — the interrogation techniques were "so offensive"

### The Voluntariness Test: Fikes v. Alabama (352 U.S. 191 [1957])
- A confession is involuntary when, under the **"totality of circumstances that preceded the confession,"** the defendant is deprived of their "power of resistance" (p. 198)
- Provides **no uniform criteria** → courts take a **case-by-case** approach
- Two recurring issues: **(1)** the police conduct, **(2)** the characteristics of the accused

### Police Conduct
- **Physical brutality to coerce a confession violates the Fourteenth Amendment**
  - Justice Douglas, *Williams v. United States*, 341 U.S. 97 [1951]: "Where police take matters into their own hands, seize victims, and beat them until they confess, they deprive the victims of rights under the Constitution" (p. 101)
- Conduct below the level of torture can still be questionable:
  - *Rogers v. Richmond*, 365 U.S. 534 [1963] — confessed after police said they would take his wife into custody → **coerced**
  - *Lynumn v. Illinois*, 372 U.S. 528 [1963] — confessed after being promised leniency → **coerced**
- **Deception alone does not necessarily** produce an involuntary confession — it is one factor among several
- Working rule: psychological pressure, promises of leniency, and deception are **rarely by themselves enough**, but **two or more** such acts (**especially coupled with physical force**) will more than likely yield an involuntary confession

### Characteristics of the Accused
- **Haley v. Ohio (332 U.S. 596 [1948])** — reversed the conviction of a 15-year-old; quoted: "Mature men possibly might stand the ordeal from midnight to 5 a.m. but we cannot believe that a lad of tender years is a match for the police in such a contest" (pp. 599–600)
- **Fatigue and pain** (e.g., from injury) can render a statement involuntary, but this usually requires **questionable conduct** by questioners:
  - *Ashcraft v. Tennessee*, 322 U.S. 143 [1944]
  - *Mincey v. Arizona*, 437 U.S. 385 [1978]
  - *Beecher v. Alabama*, 408 U.S. 234 [1972]

### The General Rule
Voluntariness is overcome when **both**:
1. The police subject the suspect to **coercive conduct**, **and**
2. That conduct is **sufficient to overcome the will** of the suspect

The second requirement looks to the **totality of the circumstances** — whether the suspect's vulnerabilities and condition, coupled with the police conduct, led to an involuntary confession (*Colorado v. Connelly*, 479 U.S. 157 [1986]).

#### Figure 8-1 — Factors Considered in Determining Voluntariness

**Police Behavior**
- Psychological pressure by the police
- Promises of leniency
- Deception
- Length of detention
- Duration of questioning
- Intensity of questioning
- Deprivation of access to family, friends, nourishment, and counsel
- Whether the suspect was advised of his or her rights

**Characteristics of the Suspect**
- Disability
- Immaturity
- Intoxication
- Fatigue
- Pain
- Age
- Level of education
- Familiarity with the criminal process

#### Figure 8-2 — Supreme Court Cases Pertaining to the Voluntariness of Confessions

| Case | Holding / Facts |
|---|---|
| *Chambers v. Florida*, 309 U.S. 227 (1940) | "Confessions of murder procured by repeated inquisitions of prisoners without friends or counselors present, and under circumstances to inspire terror, held compulsory." |

## 2 - The Sixth Amendment Approach

**Massiah v. United States (377 U.S. 201 [1964])** established that the Sixth Amendment guarantee to counsel in all **"formal criminal proceedings"** is violated when the government **"deliberately elicits"** incriminating responses from a person.

Two key elements (Figure 8-3): **deliberate elicitation** + **formal criminal proceedings**

### Deliberate Elicitation
*Massiah* — defendant released on bail pending trial for federal narcotics violations made an incriminating statement in the car of a friend who had allowed the government to install a radio in the car. Justice Stewart for the majority:
- If the right to counsel is "to have any efficacy it must apply to indirect and surreptitious interrogations as well as those conducted in the jailhouse" (p. 206)
- "Massiah [the defendant] was more seriously imposed upon . . . because he did not even know that he was under interrogation by a government agent" (p. 206)

*Brewer v. Williams (430 U.S. 387 [1977])* — defendant suspected of killing a 10-year-old girl; his attorneys told him to make no statements during the police transport, and police promised not to question him. During the trip an officer mentioned the girl deserved a "Christian burial" and that an incoming snowstorm would make the body hard to find, then said "I do not want you to answer me. I don't want to discuss it further. Just think about it as we're riding down the road" (p. 432). Williams then admitted the killing and led police to the body.
- Court reversed: the officer had "deliberately and designedly set out to elicit information from Williams . . . just as surely as—and perhaps more effectively than—if he had formally interrogated him" (p. 399)

*United States v. Henry (447 U.S. 264 [1980])* — officers "intentionally creat[ed] a situation likely to induce Henry [the defendant] to make incriminating statements without the assistance of counsel" (p. 274)
- **Nichols**, a fellow inmate, was enlisted to be alert to any statements about a robbery
- Police did **not** ask Nichols to start a conversation — only to listen
- Officers created a situation likely to elicit a response, but **only because Nichols was a paid informant**

*Kuhlmann v. Wilson (477 U.S. 436 [1986])* — **Contrast.** Kuhlmann, the informant, asked **no** questions about the crime; he only listened to (and later reported) the defendant's "spontaneous" and "unsolicited" statements. No deliberate elicitation.
- The line between *Henry* and *Kuhlmann* is **thin**. The only distinction is that Nichols had worked with the police in the past **and was being paid**

### Formal Criminal Proceedings
*Escobedo v. Illinois (378 U.S. 478 [1964])* — arrested for murder, questioned, released. Ten days later an accomplice implicated him and he was re-arrested. He requested to consult his attorney; the request was denied. Convicted of murder based partly on the accomplice's statement. Supreme Court reversed:
> "We hold . . . that where, as here, the investigation is no longer a general inquiry into an unsolved crime but has begun to focus on a particular suspect, the suspect has been taken into police custody, the police carry out a process of interrogations that lends itself to eliciting incriminating statements, the suspect has requested and been denied an opportunity to consult with his lawyer, and the police have not effectively warned him of his absolute constitutional right to remain silent, the accused has been denied the 'Assistance of Counsel' in violation of the Sixth Amendment . . . and that no statement elicited by the police during the interrogation may be used against him at a criminal trial." (pp. 490–491)

- **Escobedo caused significant confusion**: *Massiah* held the right applies once formal proceedings begin; *Escobedo* seemed to broaden it to the point where the accused becomes the focus of an investigation. This left open: **when does a person become an "accused"?**

**When do formal criminal proceedings commence?**
- *Massiah* was **indicted**, so many courts concluded formal proceedings begin with **indictment** (*United States ex rel. Forella v. Follette*, 405 F.2d 680 [2d Cir. 1969])
- ***Kirby v. Illinois (406 U.S. 682 [1972])*** (8 years after *Massiah*) — the Sixth Amendment is implicated whenever the "adverse positions of the government and defendant have solidified" so that "a defendant finds himself faced with the prosecutorial forces of organized society, and is immersed in the intricacies of substantive and procedural criminal law" (p. 689). Applies "whether by way of formal charge, preliminary hearing, indictment, information, or arraignment" (p. 689, emphasis added)
- ***Rothgery v. Gillespie County (554 U.S. 1 [2008])*** — the right to counsel can attach at the **initial appearance**

**Massiah does not apply simply because a suspect has retained counsel**
- *Moran v. Burbine* (475 U.S. 412 [1986]) — what matters is whether "the government's role [has] shift[ed] from investigation to accusation" (p. 430)
- *Maine v. Moulton* (474 U.S. 159 [1985]) — excluding evidence on charges to which the right had **not** attached, merely because **other** charges were pending, would "unnecessarily frustrate the public's interest in the investigation of criminal activities" (p. 180)

### The Approach Is Offense-Specific
*Texas v. Cobb (531 U.S. 162 [2001])* — indicted for **burglary** and given access to counsel (prohibiting deliberate elicitation). He confessed to **murdering** the woman and child in the home he burglarized. He sought exclusion; the Court disagreed — the burglary charge did not trigger Sixth Amendment protection for the murder charge.

### Waiver of the Sixth Amendment Right to Counsel
- ***Michigan v. Jackson (475 U.S. 625 [1986])*** — once an accused has **asserted** their Sixth Amendment right to counsel, statements from **subsequent** questioning are inadmissible unless the **accused initiated** the communication
- **Overruled by *Montejo v. Louisiana (556 U.S. 778 [2009])***
  - Unbeknownst to police, Montejo had been **appointed** an attorney; a detective encouraged him to write a letter of apology to the victim's wife
  - He was advised of his **Miranda** rights, but counsel was not present when he wrote it
  - The prosecution introduced the letter; Montejo moved to exclude it
  - Court disagreed — **practical meaning**: after reading Miranda rights and receiving a **voluntary waiver**, law enforcement may interrogate a suspect who has been appointed counsel, provided the suspect either (1) has not previously asserted Miranda protection, or (2) has asserted it and subsequently waives it
  - **Beneficial to law enforcement** — more opportunities to secure incriminating statements

> The Sixth and Fourteenth Amendments still apply in certain situations. If the police conduct is **not** a custodial interrogation but **formal charges have been filed**, the Sixth Amendment applies. If custody and interrogation do not occur and no formal charges are filed, the **due process voluntariness** test can still be relevant. Think of the Fourteenth Amendment as a **fallback** — if no other constitutional protection applies, due process almost always does.

## 3 - The Miranda Approach

### Malloy v. Hogan (378 U.S. 1 [1964])
A "very important yet frequently overlooked case." The Fifth Amendment's self-incrimination clause **applies to the states**:
> "today the admissibility of a confession in a state criminal prosecution is tested by the same standard applied in federal prosecution since 1897" (p. 7)

### Miranda v. Arizona (384 U.S. 436 [1966])
Core rule:
> "[T]he prosecution may not use statements, whether exculpatory or inculpatory, stemming from custodial interrogation of the defendant unless it demonstrates the use of procedural safeguards effective to secure the privilege against self-incrimination" (p. 444, emphasis added)

Facts: On March 13, 1963, **Ernesto Miranda** was arrested at home and taken to a Phoenix police station, where he was identified by the complaining witness. Police took him to "Interrogation Room No. 2" of the detective bureau, where two officers questioned him. The officers admitted at trial he was not advised he had a right to an attorney present. Two hours later they emerged with a written confession signed by Miranda, topped with a paragraph stating it was made voluntarily, without threats or promises of immunity, and "with full knowledge of my legal rights, understanding any statement I make may be used against me."

#### Figure 8-4 — Minimum Required Miranda Warnings
1. You have the right to remain silent.
2. Anything you say can and will be used against you in court.
3. You have the right to talk with an attorney and to have an attorney present before and during any questioning.
4. If you cannot afford an attorney, one will be appointed free of charge to represent you before and during any questioning.

### Miranda Is Only a Rule of Admissibility
- Often misread as a **code of conduct** for police interrogation. It is **only a rule governing admissibility of evidence**
- Separation of powers forbids the judiciary from setting policy for the executive branch
- *United States v. Patane* (542 U.S. 630 [2004]): "the police do not violate a suspect's constitutional rights (or the Miranda rule) by negligent or even deliberate failures to provide full Miranda warnings. Potential violations occur, if at all, only upon the admission of unwarned statements into evidence."
- **Chavez v. Martinez, 538 U.S. 760 (2003)** — failure to give a Miranda warning is not illegal unless the case becomes a criminal one

### Miranda Requires Government Actors
- Questioning must be conducted by **government actors**. If the people questioning are not government actors, Fifth Amendment protections do not apply
- **However**: when a private individual conducts a **custodial interrogation as an agent of** (working for) the police, **Miranda applies**

### Custody and Interrogation Are Both Required
When **neither** custody **nor** interrogation (or both) occur, Miranda does not apply.

*Salinas v. Texas (570 U.S. [2013])* — a suspect who was neither in custody nor "interrogated" answered an officer's questions about a murder but then fell silent when asked more detailed questions; the prosecution used the silence as proof of guilt. The Court approved: "petitioner's Fifth Amendment claim fails because he did not expressly invoke the privilege in response to the officer's question."

> **Common misconception:** Miranda rights do **not** apply whenever police begin questioning a person. Simple police questioning — or even a full-blown interrogation — is not enough.

### Custody

Miranda applies when "a person has been taken into custody or otherwise deprived of his freedom of action in any significant way."

- An **arrest** is a clear-cut case of custody, but lesser intrusions are harder
- No easy answer → courts focus on the **circumstances of each case**
- Standard: "the only relevant inquiry [in analyzing the custody issue] is how a **reasonable man in the suspect's position** would have understood his situation" (*Berkemer v. McCarty*, 468 U.S. 420 [1984], p. 442)

#### Six Types of Police/Citizen Encounters Analyzed for Custody

**1. Traffic and field stops → NONCUSTODIAL**
- ***Berkemer v. McCarty (468 U.S. 420 [1984])*** — motorist stopped for weaving in and out of traffic; admitted drinking and smoking marijuana, then arrested. He argued he should have been advised of the right to remain silent. Court disagreed — vehicle stops are "presumptively temporary and brief" and sufficiently public to avoid the appearance of being coercive
- A single officer asked "a modest number of questions and requested him to perform a simple balancing test at a location visible to passing motorists" (p. 442)
- The same applies to non-vehicle stops: Miranda permits "[g]eneral on-the-scene questioning as to facts surrounding a crime or other general questioning of citizens in the factfinding process" (*Yarborough v. Alvarado*, 541 U.S. 652 [2004], p. 477)
- For **Terry stops** specifically: "[t]he comparatively nonthreatening character of [investigative] detentions explains the absence of any suggestion in our opinions that Terry stops are subject to the dictates of Miranda" (*Berkemer*, p. 440)
- **But:** if a stop becomes more intrusive than a Terry stop — longer in duration and/or in a private setting — the Fifth Amendment will usually apply

**2. Questioning in the home → CONTEXT-DEPENDENT**
- ***Orozco v. Texas (394 U.S. 324 [1969])*** — four officers woke a man in his **own home** and began questioning → **custody existed**
- ***Beckwith v. United States (425 U.S. 341 [1976])*** — **contrast:** IRS agents interviewed a man in his home → **noncustodial**. He argued that being the focus of a criminal investigation required advisement. Chief Justice Burger: "Miranda specifically defined 'focus,' for its purposes, as 'questioning initiated by law enforcement officers after a person has been taken into custody or otherwise deprived of his freedom of action in any significant way'" (p. 347)

**3. Questioning at the police station → CONTEXT-DEPENDENT**
- ***Oregon v. Mathiason (429 U.S. 492 [1977])*** — man voluntarily agreed to meet officers at the station:
> "Any interview of one suspected of a crime by a police officer will have coercive aspects to it, simply by virtue of the fact that the police officer is part of a law enforcement system which may ultimately cause the suspect to be charged with a crime. But police officers are not required to administer Miranda warnings to everyone whom they question. Nor is the requirement of warnings to be imposed simply because the questioning takes place in the stationhouse, or because the questioned person is one whom the police suspect." (p. 495)
- ***California v. Beheler (463 U.S. 1121 [1983])*** — Miranda is not implicated "if the suspect is not placed under arrest, voluntarily comes to the police station, and is allowed to leave unhindered by the police after a brief interview" (p. 1121)
  - Note: *Beheler* seems to hold **even if the person is pressured** to come to the station (see *Yarborough v. Alvarado*, 541 U.S. 652 [2004])

**4. Questioning of juveniles → AGE-DEPENDENT**
- ***J.D.B. v. North Carolina (564 U.S. [2011])*** — "a reasonable child subjected to police questioning will sometimes feel pressured to submit when a reasonable adult would feel free to go." Officers questioned a boy in a school conference room about an alleged digital camera theft; he confessed; conviction reversed

**5. Probation officer / probationer meetings → NONCUSTODIAL**
- ***Minnesota v. Murphy, 465 U.S. 420 (1984)*** — probationer confessed to rape and murder during a required meeting; argued he should have been advised
  - His "freedom of movement [was] not restricted to the degree associated with formal arrest"
  - "Custodial arrest is said to convey to the suspect a message that he has no choice but to submit to the officers' will and to confess . . . [i]t is unlikely that a probation interview, arranged by appointment at a mutually convenient time, would give rise to a similar impression" (p. 433)
  - "Many of the psychological ploys discussed in *Miranda* capitalize on the suspect's unfamiliarity with the officers and the environment. Murphy's regular meetings with his probation officer should have served to familiarize him with her and her office and to insulate him from psychological intimidation that might overbear his desire to claim the privilege. Finally, the coercion inherent in custodial interrogation derives in large measure from an interrogator's insinuation that the interrogation will continue until a confession is obtained . . ."
  - Not physically restrained and able to leave → any compulsion from the threat of revoking probation "was not comparable to the pressure on a suspect who is painfully aware that he literally cannot escape a persistent custodial interrogator" (p. 433)

**6. Questioning for minor crimes → NO DISTINCTION**
- ***Berkemer*** also asked whether a Miranda exception should exist for misdemeanors. The Court declared that **no distinction** should be drawn between types of crimes as far as Miranda is concerned
- The only relevant issue is whether a person is **in custody** (and, of course, interrogated)
- "Even for a misdemeanor, the incentive for police to try to induce the defendant to incriminate him- or herself may well be significant"

#### Figure 8-5 — Custodial and Noncustodial Situations Under Miranda

**Custodial**
- Suspect is arrested and forced to go to the police station for questioning
- Excessively lengthy confrontation
- Not free to leave
- Involuntary encounter
- Private place, such as a police station

**Noncustodial**
- Traffic stop (*Berkemer v. McCarty*, 468 U.S. 420 [1984])
- Police questioning in a person's home (*Orozco v. Texas*, 394 U.S. 324 [1969])
- Terry stop (*Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 [1968])
- Suspect voluntarily goes to the police station for questioning and is free to leave (*California v. Beheler*, 463 U.S. 1121 [1983])

#### Figure 8-6 — Distinguishing Custodial and Noncustodial Situations

| Custodial Situation | Noncustodial Situation |
|---|---|
| Arrest | Typical traffic stop |
| Excessively lengthy confrontation | General on-the-scene questioning |
| Not free to leave | Free to leave |
| Involuntary encounter | Voluntary encounter |
| Private place, such as a police station | Public place, where movement is not restricted |

### Interrogation

Custody alone is not enough — the suspect must also be **interrogated** to be afforded Fifth Amendment protection, and particularly to be advised of the right to remain silent.

- *Miranda* defined interrogation as "questioning initiated by law enforcement officers"
- ***Rhode Island v. Innis (446 U.S. 291 [1980])*** — interrogation "must reflect a measure of compulsion above and beyond that inherent in custody itself" (p. 300)
- Questions tending to incriminate — directed at an individual about their **suspected involvement in a crime** — are interrogation
- **The "functional equivalent" of a question** is also possible: "any words or actions on the part of the police (other than those normally attendant to arrest and custody) that the police should know are reasonably likely to elicit an incriminating response from the suspect" (p. 302, n. 8)
  - *Innis* facts: while driving defendant to the station after an arrest for armed robbery, officers talked **among themselves** about the danger the missing weapon posed to schoolchildren with disabilities. Defendant directed them to the location of the weapon
  - Court held this was **not** interrogation — it was "nothing more than a dialogue between the two officers to which no response from the respondent was invited" (p. 315)
  - The majority implicitly assumed suspects will not respond to "'indirect appeals to . . . humanitarian impulses'" — but Justice Stevens dissented, arguing that assumption "'is directly contrary to the teachings of police interrogation manuals, which recommend appealing to a suspect's sense of morality as a standard and often successful interrogation technique'" (p. 315)
  - **Practical effect**: *Innis* effectively **expanded** the definition — mere conversation between officers designed to elicit an incriminating response, even if **not directed at the suspect**, can require Miranda warnings. Custody is still required.

#### Figure 8-7 — Distinguishing Interrogation and General Questioning

| Interrogation | General Questioning |
|---|---|
| Guilt-seeking questions | Information-gathering questions |
| Conversation intended to elicit a response | Conversation not intended to elicit a response |
| "Express questioning" or its functional equivalent | Casual conversation |

### Substance and Adequacy of the Warnings
- ***California v. Prysock (453 U.S. 355 [1981])*** — juvenile was told he had the right to talk to a lawyer before questioning and have him present, and that he had the right to a **court-appointed** lawyer, but was **not told one would be provided if he was indigent**. Court found the warnings sufficient: "Miranda itself indicates that no talismanic incantation was required to satisfy its strictures" (p. 359)
- ***Duckworth v. Eagan (492 U.S. 192 [1989])*** — **5-to-4**. The warnings (full text quoted in the case) said counsel would be provided "if and when you go to court." Held the warnings "touched all the bases required by Miranda" (p. 203). Simple departures are fine **as long as all the essential information is communicated**
- ***Colorado v. Spring (479 U.S. 564 [1987])*** — extra/unnecessary information. Arrested and questioned on suspicion of transporting stolen firearms; also questioned about a homicide. Statements about the homicide were inadmissible because he had not been informed he would be questioned about the homicide. Majority held "a suspect's awareness of all the possible subjects of questioning in advance of interrogation is not relevant to determining whether the suspect voluntarily, knowingly, and intelligently waived his Fifth Amendment privilege" (p. 577)
- ***Florida v. Powell (559 U.S. [2010])*** — warning added: "You have the right to use any of these rights at any time you want during this interview." Held this **conformed** with *Miranda* — it is sufficient to advise the suspect of the right to talk with a lawyer before answering questions and to consult a lawyer at any time during questioning

#### Figure 8-8 — San Bernardino Police Department Miranda Advisement Policy
Most departments have a policy prescribing exact wording. A sample policy:
- The warning should always be **read to the suspect** rather than relying on memory
- After the warning, ask two questions and secure an affirmative reply to each; record the **exact words** the defendant uses in response:
  1. "Do you understand the rights I have just explained to you?"
  2. "With these rights in mind, do you wish to talk to me/us now?"
- The policy also includes a **Spanish-language** version of both the warning and the waiver questions

### Waiver of Miranda
> "a heavy burden rests on the government to demonstrate that the defendant knowingly and intelligently waived his privilege against self-incrimination and his right to retained or appointed counsel" (p. 475)
> "a valid waiver will not be presumed simply from the silence of the accused after warnings are given or simply from the fact that a confession was in fact eventually obtained" (p. 475)

Evidence that a waiver was **not** made:
> "the fact of lengthy interrogation or incommunicado incarceration before a statement is made is strong evidence that the accused did not validly waive his rights... Moreover, any evidence that the accused was threatened, tricked, or cajoled into a waiver will, of course, show that the defendant did not voluntarily waive his privilege." (p. 476)

**Courts have since interpreted this language loosely:**
- *Colorado v. Connelly* — the government need only show validity by a **"preponderance of evidence"**
- ***Fare v. Michael C. (439 U.S. 1310 [1978])*** — the "totality of the circumstances approach is adequate to determine whether there has been a waiver" (p. 725)
- ***Berghuis v. Thompkins (560 U.S. 370 [2010])*** — "where the prosecution shows that a Miranda warning was given and that it was understood by the accused, an accused's uncoerced statement establishes an **implied waiver** of the right to remain silent." Simply remaining silent does **not** imply invocation of Miranda protection

**Voluntariness of the waiver** — similar to the due process voluntariness test. Threats, physical force, and the like can produce involuntary waivers. In *Fare v. Michael C.*, the Court held a confession obtained from a **16-year-old** was not involuntary; Justice Powell's strongly worded dissent argued the juvenile "was immature, emotional, and uneducated, and therefore was likely to be vulnerable to the skillful, two-on-one, repetitive style of interrogation to which he was subjected" (p. 733).
- **Safe rule**: police must engage in **seriously questionable conduct** for the voluntariness requirement of a Miranda waiver to be violated
- **Common practice**: many departments require each suspect to complete a **written Miranda waiver before interrogation commences** to ensure the waiver is documented

#### Figure 8-9 — Miranda Waiver Form (sample, San Bernardino PD)
A signed waiver form restating the four rights, plus: "I understand these rights. These rights have been explained to me. With these rights in mind, I am willing to talk to officers about the charges against me," with date, witness, and signature lines.
- **A signed written waiver is not required by law** — the law only requires that the waiver be free, intelligent, and voluntary

### Questioning After Assertion of the Right to Remain Silent
As a general rule, questioning must **cease** once the accused asserts the right to remain silent — but there is at least one exception.

- ***Michigan v. Mosley (423 U.S. 96 [1975])*** — two hours after the defendant said he did not want to talk, a **different officer** confronted him in a **different room** about **another crime** and re-read Miranda. He then made incriminating statements. The Court held his rights had been "scrupulously honored" because police "immediately ceased the interrogation, resumed questioning only after the passage of a significant period of time and the provision of a fresh set of warnings, and restricted the second interrogation to a crime that had not been a subject of the earlier interrogation" (p. 106)
- ***Bobby v. Dixon (565 U.S. [2011])*** — police did not warn Dixon before questioning him about a suspected **forgery**. In a **subsequent** interrogation he was given Miranda rights and confessed to a **murder**. Both crimes arose from the same act (burying a man alive and stealing his identity), but he was questioned separately about each. Conviction upheld: "Dixon received Miranda warnings before confessing to Hammer's murder; the effectiveness of those warnings was not impaired by the sort of 'two-step interrogation technique' . . ."
- **The common thread** in *Mosley* and *Dixon*: the second set of questions either involved a **separate crime** or led to a confession to a **second crime**
- ***Maryland v. Shatzer (559 U.S. 98 [2010])*** — **the same crime** is OK if enough time has passed. Police resumed questioning about the same crime more than **two weeks** after release; he was re-read Miranda and confessed to sex abuse crimes. Admissible, in part because "[h]is change of heart [was] . . . likely attributable to the fact that further deliberation in familiar surroundings [had] caused him to believe (rightly or wrongly) that cooperating with the investigation [was] in his interest"
- If police had continued asking about the same crime **immediately**, the questioning would have been inappropriate

### The Public Safety Exception to Miranda
On some occasions, custodial interrogation is permissible **without** Miranda warnings — at least in the short term.

- ***New York v. Quarles (467 U.S. 649 [1984])***
  - **Facts**: After learning a man with a gun had entered a supermarket, Officer Kraft and three other officers entered. Kraft spotted the defendant, drew his gun, and ordered him to stop with hands over his head. A frisk turned up an **empty shoulder holster**. Asked where the gun was, he replied "The gun is over there." Kraft retrieved the revolver, then arrested him and read Miranda. The gun was excluded below as obtained in violation of Miranda
  - **Majority (Rehnquist)**: rigid application of Miranda is not always warranted
    > "[T]he need for answers to questions in a situation posing a threat to public safety outweighs the need for the prophylactic rule protecting the Fifth Amendment's privilege against self-incrimination. We decline to place officers such as Officer Kraft in the untenable position of having to consider, often in a matter of seconds, whether it best serves society for them to ask the necessary questions without the Miranda warnings and render whatever probative evidence they uncover inadmissible, or for them to give the warnings in order to preserve the admissibility of evidence they might uncover but possibly damage or destroy their ability to obtain that evidence and neutralize the volatile situation confronting them." (pp. 657–658)
  - The test is **objective**: "Where spontaneity rather than adherence to a police manual is necessarily the order of the day, the application of the [public safety] exception . . . should not be made to depend on post hoc findings at a suppression hearing concerning the subjective motivation of the arresting officer" (p. 656)
  - The officers **did not know where the gun was located**, so not knowing "obviously posed more than one danger to the public safety: an accomplice might make use of it [or] a customer or employee might later come upon it" (p. 657)
- **Dissent (O'Connor)** — *Miranda* has never been read to prohibit police from asking questions to secure public safety; the critical question is **who bears the cost** when such questions are asked and answered
    > "Quarles, by contrast, appears to place the burden on the defendant. It does so, in my view, not by ensuring that public safety is preserved but by creating a Miranda loophole that helps ensure that otherwise inadmissible evidence can be used against the defendant." (p. 664)

### Challenging Miranda
- In **1968**, Congress passed the **Crime Control Act**, attempting to overrule *Miranda*. Codified at **18 U.S.C. § 3501**: in any federal prosecution, a confession "shall be admissible in evidence if it is voluntarily given." Suspects are **not** required to be advised of their rights under the statute
- The provision sat **dormant** for years — U.S. attorneys general knew that invoking it would challenge the Supreme Court
- ***Dickerson v. United States (530 U.S. 428 [2000])*** — Charles Dickerson was indicted for bank robbery; moved to suppress an FBI statement made without Miranda warnings. The 4th Circuit held § 3501 governed instead of *Miranda*. Supreme Court, **7-to-2** (Rehnquist):
    > "We hold that Miranda, being a constitutional decision of this Court, may not be in effect overruled by an Act of Congress, and we decline to overrule Miranda ourselves. We therefore hold that Miranda and its progeny in this Court govern the admissibility of statements made during custodial interrogation in both state and federal courts." (p. 431)

### Figure 8-10 — Relationship Between the Various Approaches to Confession Law

| Approach | Requirements |
|---|---|
| **Due Process Voluntariness** | Involuntary under totality of circumstances |
| **Miranda** | Custody **and** Interrogation |
| **Sixth Amendment** | Formal charges **and** Deliberate elicitation |

All three approaches may be applicable simultaneously.

### Key Miranda-Line Decisions Since 2000

| Year | Case | Holding |
|---|---|---|
| 2003 | *Chavez v. Martinez*, 538 U.S. 760 | Failure to give a Miranda warning is not illegal unless the case becomes a criminal one. |
| 2003 | *United States v. Patane*, 542 U.S. 630 | A voluntary statement given absent a Miranda warning can be used to obtain evidence usable at trial. Failure to warn does not make seizure of evidence illegal *per se*. |
| 2010 | *Florida v. Powell*, 559 U.S. | Warnings need not advise the suspect of the right to have an attorney **present** during questioning. Sufficient to advise the right to talk with a lawyer before questioning and to consult a lawyer at any time during questioning. |
| 2010 | *Maryland v. Shatzer*, 559 U.S. 98 | Miranda protections do not apply if a suspect is released from custody for at least **14 days** and then questioned. If re-arrested, Miranda warnings must be read. |
| 2010 | *Berghuis v. Thompkins*, 560 U.S. 370 | Unless a suspect **asserts** Miranda rights, any subsequent voluntary statements after the warnings are admissible. Simply remaining silent does not imply Miranda protection has been invoked. |
| 2011 | *J.D.B. v. North Carolina*, 564 U.S. | Children may be more prone to confessing to crimes they did not commit; age factors into the custody analysis. |

## 4 - The Exclusionary Rule and Confessions

The exclusionary rule is often said to apply **only** to the Fourth Amendment — partly because the Fifth Amendment essentially contains its **own** exclusionary rule by prohibiting compulsion of testimony. The debate is "largely semantic"; **evidence obtained in violation of any constitutional amendment will not be admissible** in a criminal trial.

Generally, a confession obtained in violation of Miranda or another constitutional provision **will** be excluded. But an illegally obtained confession does **not** mean that all subsequently obtained evidence is automatically excluded.

### Confessions and Impeachment
Improperly obtained incriminating statements may be admissible when used for **impeachment** (attacking the credibility of a witness in court). Two restrictions:
1. The statement must have been obtained **voluntarily** in the due process sense
2. The rules differ depending on whether it is a **Miranda** case or a **Sixth Amendment** right-to-counsel case

**Miranda violation → impeachment permitted**
- ***Harris v. New York (401 U.S. 222 [1971])*** — prosecution introduced an out-of-court statement inconsistent with Harris's in-court testimony, though obtained in violation of Miranda. Admissible **for impeachment only**, not as evidence against him. The statement must also have been voluntarily obtained
  - See also *Oregon v. Hass*, 420 U.S. 714 [1975]; *New Jersey v. Portash*, 437 U.S. 385 [1978]

**But silence is NOT an oral communication**
- A statement obtained in violation of Miranda is admissible for impeachment only if it is an **oral communication**. The prosecution **cannot** introduce evidence of an accused's **out-of-court silence** for impeachment
- ***Doyle v. Ohio (426 U.S. 610 [1976])*** — after the defendant's in-court exculpatory story, the prosecution sought to introduce evidence that he had not offered the same explanation to police. The Court held the **silence was not admissible** for impeachment
  - See also *Wainwright v. Greenfield*, 474 U.S. 284 [1986]

**Sixth Amendment violation → impeachment permitted**
- ***Michigan v. Harvey (494 U.S. 344 [1990])*** — a statement obtained in violation of the right to counsel (police questioned the defendant after he invoked counsel and later waived it) **could** be admitted for impeachment
- ***Kansas v. Ventris (556 U.S. [2009])*** — police used an informant to obtain a statement from Ventris after he had been formally charged. Admitted for **impeachment only**, not as part of the government's case in chief, even though obtained in violation of the Sixth Amendment

### Confessions and "Fruit of the Poisonous Tree"
The Supreme Court has **not** been quick to apply the fruit-of-the-poisonous-tree doctrine to illegally obtained confessions. **Physical evidence** obtained in violation of Miranda is admissible, as long as the information supplied by the accused is **voluntary in the due process sense**.

- ***United States v. Bayer (331 U.S. 532 [1947])*** — decided well before *Miranda*. The Fourth Amendment "fruit of the poisonous tree" doctrine does **not** control the admissibility of improperly obtained **confessions**
- ***Michigan v. Tucker (417 U.S. 433 [1974])*** — decided after Miranda but involving a pre-Miranda interrogation. Suspect questioned about a rape without right-to-counsel advisement; claimed he was with a friend. Police then obtained incriminating evidence **from the friend**
  - Although this information was clearly fruit of the initial interrogation, the Court stated "[t]he police conduct at issue here did not abridge respondent's constitutional privilege against compulsory self-incrimination, but departed only from the **prophylactic standards** later laid down by this Court in Miranda to safeguard this privilege" (p. 446)
  - These decisions govern **derivative evidence**, not the initial incriminating statements. The latter are inadmissible when obtained in violation of the Fifth Amendment
- ***Oregon v. Elstad (470 U.S. 298 [1985])*** — reaffirmed *Tucker*, making clear Miranda is a **prophylactic rule**, at least as it governs the admissibility of derivative evidence. Derivative evidence obtained from a Miranda violation is admissible **if voluntarily obtained**

## THE CASE: A Legitimate Public Safety Exception?

**Facts.** Dzhokhar and Tamerlan Tsarnaev detonated two homemade explosive devices at the finish line of the **2013 Boston Marathon**. Three people were killed and more than 200 injured. Tamerlan was killed during the ensuing manhunt. Dzhokhar, who survived bullet wounds and two gun battles with law enforcement, was captured and charged federally with using and conspiring to use a weapon of mass destruction and malicious destruction of property with an explosive device. On **April 8, 2015**, he was found guilty on all charges and sentenced to death. As of the writing of the text, the appeals process continues.

**Issue.** Police invoked the *Quarles* public safety exception and questioned Dzhokhar **without** advising him of his Miranda rights. He was eventually notified of his right not to incriminate himself. Although the courts have supported the need for urgent questioning in special cases, the announcement by authorities that Dzhokhar would not be advised of his rights — even though he had been hospitalized for hours after arrest and the immediate danger of more bombs seemed to have passed — surprised legal experts. Interrogators argued the need for urgency still existed, as he might have conspired with collaborators who continued to work on other attacks as he lay immobilized.

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Confession** | A person implicating him- or herself in criminal activity following police questioning and/or interrogation |
| **Admission** | A person simply admits to involvement in a crime without any police encouragement |
| **Due process voluntariness approach** | The requirement that any confession be voluntary under the "totality of circumstances" |
| **Deliberate elicitation** | In the Sixth Amendment right-to-counsel context, occurs when police officers create a situation likely to induce a suspect into making an incriminating statement |
| **Formal criminal proceeding** | In the Sixth Amendment right-to-counsel context: formal charge, preliminary hearing, indictment, information, or arraignment |
| **Custody** | Typically an arrest. Important because Miranda warnings do not need to be read if a person is not in custody |
| **Interrogation** | Express questioning (e.g., "Where were you on the night of the crime?") or the **functional equivalent of a question** |
| **Functional equivalent of a question** | "[A]ny words or actions on the part of the police (other than those normally attendant to arrest and custody) that the police should know are reasonably likely to elicit an incriminating response from the suspect" (*Rhode Island v. Innis*, 446 U.S. 291 [1980], p. 302, n. 8) |
| **18 U.S.C. § 3501** | Federal statute enacted in the wake of *Miranda* providing any confession "shall be admissible in evidence if it is voluntarily given." Deemed unconstitutional in *Dickerson v. United States* (530 U.S. 428 [2000]) |

## Think About It (Discussion Prompts)

1. **Police Conduct and Voluntariness** — A suspect was interrogated by five officers who, guns drawn, stood over him as he lay handcuffed and semiconscious from a gunshot wound he had received earlier (not inflicted by the officers). The officers did not threaten to shoot him. They simply pointed their guns at him. Assuming he confessed, would the confession be involuntary under the Fourteenth Amendment?

2. **Suspect Characteristics and Voluntariness** — Ed Hornby approached a police officer and said he had killed someone and wanted to talk. He confessed to an unsolved murder from years earlier. Before trial, Hornby moved to exclude the confession; a defense psychiatrist testified Hornby suffered from **command auditory hallucinations**, rendering him unable to resist the "voices in his head." How should the court decide? *What if* Hornby had instead become hesitant to talk, and the confession was preceded by a lengthy middle-of-the-night interrogation during which he was denied food and a desperately needed trip to the restroom?

3. **The Nature of Custody** — Carole Reynolds was held in jail awaiting trial on narcotics offenses. Unable to get her to make a statement, police placed an **undercover agent** in her cell. The two women struck up a conversation, and the agent asked "What do you do for a living?" Reynolds responded, "I'm a drug kingpin." Could the statement be used against her at trial?

4. **Were the Miranda Rights Read Properly?** — William Wentworth was interrogated while in police custody. He wore an expensive suit, lavish jewelry, and otherwise exhibited an aura of financial success. Before interrogation he was read: "You have the right to remain silent. Anything you say can and will be used against you in court. You have the right to talk with an attorney, either retained by you or appointed by the court, before giving a statement, and to have your attorney present when answering any questions." Wentworth made an incriminating statement and later moved to suppress it on the grounds that he was not informed that counsel would be provided if he was indigent.

5. **Should the Exclusionary Rule Apply?** — Police hold Jack Richter in custody and interrogate him about a gruesome murder. They do not advise him of his Miranda rights. One officer, notorious for aggressive interrogation techniques, holds a loaded .357 revolver to Richter's head and plays "Russian roulette" to obtain a confession. Richter admits to the murder and points police to the weapon. Obviously his statement is inadmissible. **What about the murder weapon?**
